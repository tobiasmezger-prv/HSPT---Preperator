A Roof That Holds Rain

passage-a1-02 · original expository

[1] When rain strikes an ordinary roof, much of the water quickly travels through gutters and into drains. On a planted roof, a layer of growing material absorbs some of that water. Plants use part of the stored moisture, and some returns to the air through evaporation. The water that does reach the drain may arrive more slowly.

[2] A planted roof is not simply a garden placed on top of a building. Designers must account for the weight of wet growing material. They also include layers that protect the roof from roots and keep water from entering the rooms below. Plants are selected for the roof's light, wind, and water conditions.

[3] These roofs can help manage runoff, but their performance varies. A roof that is already soaked cannot store as much of the next storm as a dry one. Measuring only one gentle shower would therefore give an incomplete picture. Several storms, with different conditions, provide a better test.

Counting the Quiet Visitors

passage-a1-04 · original expository

[1] A school nature club wanted to learn which flowers attracted visiting insects. Students first planned to count visitors whenever they had a free moment. Their advisor asked whether a five-minute count on a cold morning could fairly be compared with a twenty-minute count on a warm afternoon. The students revised their plan.

[2] They chose equal observation periods and recorded the weather as well as the flower patch. They also agreed on what counted as a visit: an insect had to land on a flower, not merely fly past it. Because they could not reliably identify every species, they used broad categories such as bee, butterfly, and other insect. Uncertain observations were marked rather than guessed.

[3] After two weeks, one flower patch had the highest total. The club did not call it the best flower for every garden. That patch also received more afternoon sun. Next month, the students would compare patches with more similar light conditions. Their first study had produced both a result and a better question.

Why Keep the Rough Draft?

passage-a1-06 · original argument

[1] A finished essay can make writing look like a straight path from an idea to polished sentences. A folder of drafts tells a different story. An introduction disappears, a weak example gives way to a stronger one, and a conclusion changes the writer's understanding of the original question. Keeping these earlier versions can make that work visible.

[2] This does not mean saving every accidental keystroke. A useful collection might include the first complete draft, a version after major revision, and the final piece. Beside each, the writer can note one important change and the reason for it. Such notes turn a pile of pages into evidence of decisions.

[3] Some students worry that showing early work will expose mistakes. Yet a draft is not a claim of perfection. It gives a teacher a chance to discuss strategies that the final essay alone might conceal. Judged thoughtfully, the collection rewards improvement as well as the final result.

Reading an Old Photograph

passage-a1-08 · original expository

[1] A photograph of a crowded town square seems to offer a direct window into the past. Yet the image does not explain everything it shows. People may have gathered for a market, a parade, or an announcement. Without other evidence, choosing one explanation can turn an observation into a guess.

[2] An investigator begins with details that are actually visible: a banner, a shop sign, the position of shadows. Next come questions. Can the shop name be found in a directory? Does a newspaper describe an event with the same banner? The date written on the back of the photograph may help, but it should also be checked; labels can be added much later.

[3] Even after research, some uncertainty may remain. A careful caption can distinguish what is known from what is likely. "Crowd in the square, probably during the spring fair" is more informative than an unsupported exact claim. Uncertainty, clearly stated, can make an account more trustworthy rather than less.

Making Room for Silence

passage-a1-12 · original argument

[1] A community center plans to turn an unused room into a quiet study space. Some residents would rather use every room for meetings and classes. Those activities matter, but a room without a scheduled speaker or group can serve another need: a place where people can concentrate on their own work.

[2] At home, a student may share a table with younger siblings. An adult studying for a certificate may have no separate desk. A quiet room does not have to be elaborate. Good lighting, seats, work surfaces, and clear expectations about noise may be enough. Locating it away from the music room would help.

[3] The center should begin with a trial rather than a permanent promise. For six weeks, it could record how often the room is used and invite comments. If few people come, the schedule or setup could change. A quiet room deserves a fair test, not an assumption that an unscheduled space is an unused one.

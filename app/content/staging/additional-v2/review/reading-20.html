The Unlabeled Drawer

passage-a1-01 · original fiction

[1] On her first afternoon helping at the repair cafe, Mara arranged the screwdrivers by size and wiped every table twice. Across the room, Mr. Imani opened an old radio. Mara hoped he would ask her to repair something important. Instead, he handed her a drawer full of mixed screws and asked her to sort them. She worked quickly, making neat piles according to color.

[2] When Mr. Imani returned, he picked two silver screws from the same pile. One was long and thin; the other was short and wide. "Would these do the same job?" he asked. Mara shook her head. He set a ruler beside the drawer and went back to the radio without moving her piles.

[3] Mara began again, this time comparing length, width, and the shape of each head. Later, Mr. Imani asked for a short screw with a flat head. She found it immediately. As he secured the radio cover, Mara pulled another unsorted drawer toward her. This time she left the polishing cloth on the table.

  2. She still thinks polishing is the only useful job.
  3. She intends to undo the completed sorting.
  4. She wants to hide her earlier mistake.

A Roof That Holds Rain

passage-a1-02 · original expository

[1] When rain strikes an ordinary roof, much of the water quickly travels through gutters and into drains. On a planted roof, a layer of growing material absorbs some of that water. Plants use part of the stored moisture, and some returns to the air through evaporation. The water that does reach the drain may arrive more slowly.

[2] A planted roof is not simply a garden placed on top of a building. Designers must account for the weight of wet growing material. They also include layers that protect the roof from roots and keep water from entering the rooms below. Plants are selected for the roof's light, wind, and water conditions.

[3] These roofs can help manage runoff, but their performance varies. A roof that is already soaked cannot store as much of the next storm as a dry one. Measuring only one gentle shower would therefore give an incomplete picture. Several storms, with different conditions, provide a better test.

  2. One successful storm test establishes performance in all weather.
  3. Protective layers matter only on unplanted roofs.
  4. Planted roofs can store an unlimited amount of water.

The Map with Two Routes

passage-a1-03 · original fiction

[1] Jun unfolded the map his grandfather had drawn for their walk to the old lookout. A red line climbed directly up the hill; a blue line wandered around its base before rising through the trees. Jun tapped the red line. "That one is shorter." His grandfather handed him the map. "Then you can lead."

[2] For the first ten minutes, Jun felt pleased with his choice. Then the path grew steep and loose stones shifted under his shoes. At a bend, he stopped to catch his breath. Through the trees he could see the blue route, where two walkers were moving steadily along a gentler slope. He studied the map again, noticing the closely spaced elevation lines around the red path.

[3] At the lookout, his grandfather asked which way they should return. Jun traced the blue line with one finger. "It is longer on paper," he said, "but paper does not get tired." His grandfather smiled and let Jun fold the map.

Counting the Quiet Visitors

passage-a1-04 · original expository

[1] A school nature club wanted to learn which flowers attracted visiting insects. Students first planned to count visitors whenever they had a free moment. Their advisor asked whether a five-minute count on a cold morning could fairly be compared with a twenty-minute count on a warm afternoon. The students revised their plan.

[2] They chose equal observation periods and recorded the weather as well as the flower patch. They also agreed on what counted as a visit: an insect had to land on a flower, not merely fly past it. Because they could not reliably identify every species, they used broad categories such as bee, butterfly, and other insect. Uncertain observations were marked rather than guessed.

[3] After two weeks, one flower patch had the highest total. The club did not call it the best flower for every garden. That patch also received more afternoon sun. Next month, the students would compare patches with more similar light conditions. Their first study had produced both a result and a better question.

passage-a1-05 · original fiction

Esme set down her violin case and asked how the machine would turn in the narrow course. "We can adjust it later," said Cal, reaching for the parts box.

[2] Esme almost let the question go. Instead, she put two rulers on the table, the same distance apart as the course walls. Cal placed the base between them. It fit while pointing forward, but one corner struck a ruler as he tried to turn it. For a moment, no one spoke. Then Cal pushed the parts box aside and picked up the eraser.

[3] They spent the rest of the meeting drawing a narrower base. At the next meeting, Cal left a blank space on the board under the heading "Questions before building." Esme arrived late again, but this time three teammates turned toward her before opening the parts box.

Why Keep the Rough Draft?

passage-a1-06 · original argument

[1] A finished essay can make writing look like a straight path from an idea to polished sentences. A folder of drafts tells a different story. An introduction disappears, a weak example gives way to a stronger one, and a conclusion changes the writer's understanding of the original question. Keeping these earlier versions can make that work visible.

[2] This does not mean saving every accidental keystroke. A useful collection might include the first complete draft, a version after major revision, and the final piece. Beside each, the writer can note one important change and the reason for it. Such notes turn a pile of pages into evidence of decisions.

[3] Some students worry that showing early work will expose mistakes. Yet a draft is not a claim of perfection. It gives a teacher a chance to discuss strategies that the final essay alone might conceal. Judged thoughtfully, the collection rewards improvement as well as the final result.

The Last Light

passage-a1-07 · original fiction

[1] Every evening, Arun watched a square of light appear in the workshop across the alley. His neighbor, Mrs. Bell, repaired clocks there. The tiny room held a chorus of ticks, but from Arun's window he could hear only the deep chime of the clock nearest the door. One evening the square stayed dark.

[2] The next day, a sign appeared: "Moving Saturday." Arun wanted to ask Mrs. Bell where she was going, but each time he approached, she was carrying another box. On Friday he finally knocked. "I will miss the clock," he said. Mrs. Bell looked at the bare wall by the door. "It belongs to the new library now. Perhaps you will hear it there."

[3] On Saturday, Arun helped carry the last box to her van. That evening the alley was dark again. The following Monday, he entered the library to return a book. A familiar chime rolled through the reading room. He stood still until the final note faded, then walked toward the desk with the book held a little less tightly.

Reading an Old Photograph

passage-a1-08 · original expository

[1] A photograph of a crowded town square seems to offer a direct window into the past. Yet the image does not explain everything it shows. People may have gathered for a market, a parade, or an announcement. Without other evidence, choosing one explanation can turn an observation into a guess.

[2] An investigator begins with details that are actually visible: a banner, a shop sign, the position of shadows. Next come questions. Can the shop name be found in a directory? Does a newspaper describe an event with the same banner? The date written on the back of the photograph may help, but it should also be checked; labels can be added much later.

[3] Even after research, some uncertainty may remain. A careful caption can distinguish what is known from what is likely. "Crowd in the square, probably during the spring fair" is more informative than an unsupported exact claim. Uncertainty, clearly stated, can make an account more trustworthy rather than less.

rea-a1-043 · main idea · provisional level 2

8. What is the main point of the passage?

  1. A detailed caption should avoid mentioning uncertainty.
  2. Photographs never provide useful historical information.
  3. Every handwritten label is accurate.
  4. Historical photographs should be interpreted with evidence and stated uncertainty.

The Borrowed Paddle

passage-a1-09 · original fiction

[1] Leah was certain she knew how to canoe. She had watched her older cousins cross the lake every summer, their paddles dipping in a clean rhythm. When her uncle offered a short lesson near the shore, she said she would rather go straight to the island. He placed a paddle in her hands and asked her to guide the canoe around a floating marker first.

[2] Leah paddled hard on one side. The canoe turned away from the marker. She switched sides and paddled even harder; now it swung the other way. Her uncle did not reach for the paddle. "Try less," he said. She made a smaller stroke and watched what happened. After several slow attempts, the canoe curved around the marker.

[3] The island still looked close, but Leah noticed ripples farther out where the wind crossed the open water. She rested the paddle across her knees. "Can we practice coming back to shore?" she asked. Her uncle nodded, and this time she waited for the instructions.

A Fair Test of Packaging

passage-a1-10 · original expository

[1] Two student teams designed containers to protect an egg during a short drop. Team A used folded paper supports; Team B used a bed of soft fabric. When Team A's egg survived and Team B's cracked, several observers declared paper the better material. Their teacher asked them to compare how the trials had been carried out.

[2] Team A had dropped its container from a lower shelf. Team B had used a higher one. The containers also differed in size, and only one egg had been tested in each. The result showed what happened in those two trials, but it did not isolate the effect of the cushioning material.

[3] For a new test, the teams chose matching containers and the same drop height. They planned several trials with each material and recorded whether each egg cracked. Even this study would answer a limited question: how those particular designs performed under the chosen conditions. It would not identify the best package for every fragile object.

The Window Box

passage-a1-11 · original fiction

[1] Tomas planted seeds in the window box outside his apartment and checked them before school each morning. For a week, the soil looked unchanged. His sister suggested digging up a seed to see what was wrong. Tomas reached for a spoon, then read the packet again. It said seedlings could take ten to fourteen days to appear. He put the spoon away.

[2] On the eleventh morning, a pale hook rose through the soil. By evening it had straightened into a tiny stem. Tomas marked its height on a strip of paper taped beside the box. Some days he could see no difference. On those days, he compared the newest mark with the first instead of with yesterday's.

[3] When his sister returned from a week at camp, she pointed to the leaves stretching over the box. "They grew so fast!" she said. Tomas looked at the small staircase of marks on the paper. He smiled and brought out another packet of seeds.

Making Room for Silence

passage-a1-12 · original argument

[1] A community center plans to turn an unused room into a quiet study space. Some residents would rather use every room for meetings and classes. Those activities matter, but a room without a scheduled speaker or group can serve another need: a place where people can concentrate on their own work.

[2] At home, a student may share a table with younger siblings. An adult studying for a certificate may have no separate desk. A quiet room does not have to be elaborate. Good lighting, seats, work surfaces, and clear expectations about noise may be enough. Locating it away from the music room would help.

[3] The center should begin with a trial rather than a permanent promise. For six weeks, it could record how often the room is used and invite comments. If few people come, the schedule or setup could change. A quiet room deserves a fair test, not an assumption that an unscheduled space is an unused one.

The Poster That Changed

passage-a1-13 · original fiction

[1] Inez spent Saturday painting a poster for the neighborhood cleanup. Large letters announced the date above a bright drawing of the river. She taped it inside the bakery window and stepped back proudly. On Monday, her neighbor Mr. Cho asked what time volunteers should arrive. Inez pointed at the poster, then realized the time was not there.

[2] She added the time in a corner. Later, a woman carrying groceries asked where the group would meet. Inez frowned at the crowded poster. She had used nearly all the space for the river. At home, she sketched a new version with three clear lines: when, where, and what to bring. The drawing became smaller.

[3] By Friday, the sign-up sheet beside the poster was almost full. Inez still liked her first painting better as a picture. But when a child read the new poster aloud and told his mother exactly where to go, Inez decided to keep the second version in the window.

A Trail of Small Stones

passage-a1-14 · original expository

[1] Along a footpath, a row of small stones may seem like decoration. In a restoration project, however, such a row can mark the edge of a newly planted area. It offers a visible reminder to stay on the path while young roots become established. The stones are not a wall; their usefulness depends partly on whether visitors understand them.

[2] At one park, volunteers noticed that people repeatedly crossed a planted corner to reach a bench. Adding more stones did little to change the pattern. A volunteer watched the route during several busy afternoons and saw that the shortcut was much more direct than the marked path. The team moved the path slightly and placed a short sign beside the remaining planted area.

[3] In the following weeks, fewer footprints appeared among the seedlings. The volunteers could not separate the effect of the new route from the effect of the sign because both changed together. Still, the observations suggested an important lesson: protecting a place can require understanding how people use it, not merely telling them to stop.

The Rehearsal Pause

passage-a1-15 · original fiction

[1] During rehearsal, the scene always stumbled at the same place. When Theo entered with the letter, Ada rushed her reply, and the next actor began speaking before she finished. The director stopped them three times. Each time, they tried speaking more loudly, as though volume could untangle the lines.

[2] On the fourth attempt, the director asked everyone to stand still after Theo entered. No one was to speak until Ada had looked at the letter. The silence felt enormous. Ada noticed the torn corner of the envelope, then Theo's worried face. For the first time, her reply sounded like an answer to what had happened rather than a sentence she had memorized.

[3] They tried the scene again without instructions. Theo entered, Ada looked, and the other actor waited. The pause was brief, but the scene no longer hurried past its own surprise. After rehearsal, Ada marked a small dash before her line. She had practiced the words for a week; now she had something to practice between them.

rea-a1-087 · main idea · provisional level 2

19. What does the story suggest about performing a scene?

  1. Louder speech always resolves timing problems.
  2. Listening and reacting can matter as much as speaking the words.
  3. Actors should ignore one another once lines are memorized.
  4. Pauses are useful only when someone forgets a line.

One Shelf, Many Uses

passage-a1-16 · original expository

[1] A neighborhood exchange shelf began with a simple rule: leave a useful item and take one you need. During the first month, the shelf filled with books, jars, and small tools. It also filled with things that could not be used: a cracked mug, a puzzle with missing pieces, and a lamp with a damaged cord. Volunteers spent more time sorting than they had expected.

[2] They posted a clearer guide. Items had to be clean, complete, and safe to use. Large objects required a photograph and contact information instead of being left beside the shelf. A notebook invited visitors to request things they needed. Soon a request for measuring cups was answered, while several unwanted decorations remained untouched.

[3] The volunteers began reviewing the notebook each week. They did not want the shelf to become merely a place to discard possessions. Its success depended on matching useful items with people who could use them. A smaller, changing collection turned out to serve that purpose better than a larger pile that never moved.
